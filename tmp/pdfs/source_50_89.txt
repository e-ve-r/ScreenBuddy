1. LITERATURE SEARCHBuilding the Research Foundation
1.1 What is Literature Search?
Literature search is the systematic process of finding existing research related to a research problem,
question, method, technology, or domain.
It is much more than simply typing a topic into a search engine.
A good literature search helps the researcher discover:
•what is already known,
•what methods have already been used,
•which datasets and benchmarks are available,
•what results have already been achieved,
•where researchers disagree, and
•what questions remain unanswered.
LEARN & REMEMBER
Research does not start with “What can I build?”
It should first ask:
What has already been done, what is missing, and what can I contribute?
1.2 Why Literature Search Matters
Literature search:
1. helps avoid unnecessary duplication;
2. supports the novelty of a research problem;
3. identifies useful tools, datasets and benchmarks;
4. provides background for the research problem;
5. helps formulate research questions;
6. provides evidence for the proposed methodology;
7. identifies gaps and unresolved issues.

3. LITERATURE REVIEWFrom Papers to Understanding
3.1 Objectives of a Literature Review
A literature review should not become a list of paper summaries.
It should:
•summarize current knowledge,
•compare methods and results,
•identify research gaps,
•highlight contradictions or debates,
•establish the theoretical and methodological foundation of the study.
LEARN & REMEMBER
Weak literature review:
“Paper A did this. Paper B did this. Paper C did this.”
Stronger literature review:
“Most studies use machine learning for intrusion detection; however, real-time deployment remains
insufficiently explored because of computational and scalability constraints.”

7. SEARCH STRATEGYSearch, Screen, Record
7.1 Screening Search Results
Do not download every result.
First examine:
1. Title
2. Abstract
3. Keywords
4. Publication year
5. Venue
6. Relevance to your research question
Only then decide whether the full paper deserves detailed reading.
COMMON TRAP
A common mistake is downloading hundreds of papers and reading none of them carefully. Quality of
analysis is more important than the number of PDFs collected.

12. LITERATURE MAPPINGSeeing the Big Picture
12.1 What is Literature Mapping?
Literature mapping is the process of systematically or visually organizing research papers to identify:
•themes,
•clusters,
•influential studies,
•relationships between topics,
•research trends,
•research gaps.
12.2 Why Map Literature?
When a researcher has 30, 50 or 100 papers, a simple list becomes difficult to understand.
A map helps answer:
What are the major themes?
Which papers are connected?
How has the topic evolved?
Where is the research gap?

Mapping Type Description

15. REFERENCES AND CITATIONSAcademic Credibility
15.1 Why References Matter
References serve several purposes:
•acknowledge original ideas,
•allow readers to verify claims,
•help avoid plagiarism,
•establish scholarly context,
•strengthen the credibility of research.
15.2 Citation Styles
Style Typical Approach
IEEE Numbered citations such as [1], [2]
APA Author–year such as (Smith, 2020)
ACM Author–year or numeric depending on venue
Chicago / Harvard Commonly used outside core CS venues
LEARN & REMEMBER
In computer science, IEEE and ACM styles are particularly common. Always check the specific journal,
conference or university guidelines before formatting references.
15.3 Citation in Writing
Examples:
Direct citation:
According to Smith [1], deep learning has applications in NLP.
Indirect citation:
Deep learning has been widely applied to natural language processing [1].
Multiple citations:
Several studies have investigated this problem [1]–[4].
COMMON TRAP
Do not mix IEEE, APA and other citation formats randomly within the same document.

18. PLAGIARISMAcademic Integrity
18.1 What is Plagiarism?
Plagiarism means using another person’s words, ideas or work without appropriate acknowledgment.
It can involve:
•written text,
•figures,
•diagrams,
•computer code,
•datasets,
•ideas,
•previously published work.
Plagiarism may be intentional or accidental.
18.2 Types of Plagiarism
Type Description
Direct / Verbatim Copying text word-for-word
Mosaic Combining copied phrases with your own wording
Self-Plagiarism Reusing previous work without proper acknowledgment
Paraphrasing Plagiarism Changing words while retaining the original structure
Accidental Plagiarism Incorrect or missing citation
18.3 Plagiarism in Computer Science
Examples include:
•copying code from GitHub without credit,
•using another researcher’s figure without citation,
•submitting the same project for two courses,
•reusing a previous publication as a new work without proper disclosure.
COMMON TRAP
Plagiarism is not limited to copied paragraphs. Figures, code, data and ideas may also require acknowl-
edgment.

20. PARAPHRASINGReword + Restructure + Cite
20.1 What is Paraphrasing?
Paraphrasing means expressing another author’s idea in your own words and sentence structure while
preserving the original meaning.
Good paraphrasing requires:
1. understanding the source,
2. writing the idea independently,
3. changing the structure,
4. preserving the meaning,
5. citing the source.
20.2 Example
Original:
“Machine learning models require large labeled datasets for training.”
Poor paraphrasing:
“Machine learning systems need big labeled datasets for training.”
The sentence structure remains almost the same.
Better paraphrasing:
Effective machine-learning approaches often depend on extensive annotated datasets during
model development [1].
COMMON TRAP
Simply replacing words with synonyms is not sufficient. The idea must be understood and expressed
independently.
20.3 Practice
Original:
“Cloud computing improves scalability but raises security concerns.”
Write your own version.

22. RESEARCH ETHICSResponsible Conduct of Research
22.1 What is Research Ethics?
Research ethics refers to principles that guide responsible conduct during research.
It promotes:
•honesty,
•transparency,
•fairness,
•responsibility,
•protection of participants,
•reliability of the scientific record.
In computer science, ethics can involve:
•software,
•algorithms,
•datasets,
•artificial intelligence,
•human-computer interaction,
•user studies.
22.2 Core Ethical Principles
Principle Meaning

Objectivity Avoid inappropriate bias in research design and interpretation
Integrity Follow professional and institutional guidelines

Accountability Take responsibility for research work and contributions

It may also be referred to as an ethics committee.
Its role includes reviewing research involving human participants to ensure that:
•participants are treated appropriately,
•risks are considered,
•consent is handled properly,
•privacy is protected,
•ethical requirements are satisfied.
28.2 When May Approval Be Needed?
Examples include:
•surveys involving people,
•interviews,
•experiments involving participants,
•HCI studies,
•software usability studies,
•studies involving sensitive datasets,
•health, financial or biometric data,
•identifiable personal information.
COMMON TRAP
Do not assume that a project is “only computer science” and therefore automatically exempt from ethical
review. Human participants and identifiable or sensitive data can introduce ethical requirements.

29.1 Informed Consent
Participants should understand relevant information about the study, including its purpose, procedures

29.2 Confidentiality
Personal information should be protected and not unnecessarily disclosed.
29.3 Minimal Risk
Researchers should minimize avoidable harm, discomfort or risk.
29.4 Right to Withdraw
Where applicable, participants should be able to leave a study without inappropriate consequences.
29.5 Fair Selection
Participant recruitment should be fair and should not create unjustified discrimination or exploitation.
